# Signac-Like Large Dots / Short Strokes

Signac-like Pointillism more often uses larger dots, short strokes, or open color blocks, making the surface brighter, lighter, and more rhythmic than Seurat's. Britannica's Paul Signac entry can be used as an artist reference for his role in Neo-Impressionism, Pointillism, divided color, and the spread of color theory. [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Paul-Signac) This direction still belongs to Pointillist / Neo-Impressionist context, but it is not always made from round dots. Signac's harbors, seascapes, and southern light often make divided color more decorative and chromatic.

When identifying it, ask whether the touches are larger, more open, and brighter than Seurat's, and whether water, sails, buildings, and sky form a lively rhythm. The Met's Neo-Impressionism exhibition resource can be used as a reference for Signac's circle, dotted color, and divided-color method. [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2001/paul-signac-circle) Dots or short strokes may have direction; from afar, light and color blend, while up close they remain separated. If the image has only random thick strokes and no optical color separation, it should not be assigned to this direction.

Emotionally, this direction usually has positive valence and medium to medium-high arousal. Bright complementary colors, shimmering water, and open composition bring travel, air, pleasure, and light. Khan Academy's Neo-Impressionist color theory resource can be used for complementary adjacency and optical mixing as a reference for enhanced brightness. [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/becoming-modern/avant-garde-france/post-impressionism/a/neo-impressionist-color-theory) If the colors are extremely intense, the image may also create visual stimulation close to Fauvist excitement.
