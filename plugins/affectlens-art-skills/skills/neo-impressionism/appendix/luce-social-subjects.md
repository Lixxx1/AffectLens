# Luce and Social-Subject Direction

Artists such as Maximilien Luce applied Neo-Impressionist technique to workers, industry, streets, docks, night scenes, and social subjects. In this direction, divided color serves not only light but also modern social observation. The Art Story's Maximilien Luce material can serve as a reference for his worker, urban, political, and social subjects. Luce's links to anarchism and worker imagery can make his works emotionally heavier or more critical than Seurat/Signac leisure landscapes.

## Recognition

Look for factories, workers, streets, docks, night scenes, industrial smoke, or poor districts combined with divided dots or short strokes. The Met's Neo-Impressionism exhibition material can serve as a reference for divided color and pointillist practice around Seurat and Signac. These paintings may be bright and socially charged at once: optical color creates visual vitality, while subject matter brings labor, class, and urban pressure.

## Emotion and Valence/Arousal

Valence is often complex. Bright divided color can bring hope and liveliness, but industrial smoke, fatigue, crowding, and labor subjects can introduce negative or critical feeling. Khan Academy's color theory material can explain how bright divided color affects emotional brightness. Arousal depends on crowds, machines, night light, and color contrast. In analysis, name both technique and subject so dotted color does not make the emotion seem too light.
