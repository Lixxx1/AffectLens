# Divisionism

Divisionism emphasizes the principle of divided color rather than a round-dot appearance. It separates color into adjacent pure-color strokes, small blocks, short marks, or dots, letting the viewer's eye complete the mixture. Khan Academy's Neo-Impressionist color theory material is a useful reference for divided color, optical mixture, and scientific color theory. Signac stressed that Neo-Impressionism was not merely dotting but dividing color. The Met likewise notes that Divisionism is color separation, while Pointillism is a precise dotted application.

## Recognition

Look for whether brushwork systematically separates color. It may take the form of short strokes, small blocks, square marks, linear touches, or dots, not only round points. The image is usually more ordered than Impressionism, with more controlled stroke direction and color juxtaposition. If color mixing mainly happens on the palette, or brushwork is freely spontaneous, do not call it Divisionism.

## Emotion and Valence/Arousal

Divisionism can feel more open than strict Pointillism. Short-stroke direction moves the eye; bright divided color raises luminosity and positive valence; systematic separation creates rational order. Arousal depends on complementary-color intensity, stroke direction, and surface density. Signac-like harbors, sails, and southern light often feel especially bright and active. Britannica's Paul Signac entry can support his role in spreading Neo-Impressionist and divisionist theory.
