# Buddhist / Daoist Religious Painting

## Definition And Historical Context

Buddhist / Daoist religious painting includes images of Buddhas, bodhisattvas, arhats, heavenly kings, deities, Daoist immortals, guardian figures, paradises or pure lands, ritual images, and religious stories. National Palace Museum's "Figure Painting: Deities and Spirits" material notes that images depicting Buddhist figures, Daoist figures, and other spirits have been popular since the Six Dynasties. [National Palace Museum](https://theme.npm.edu.tw/PaintingsinNPM11206/en/page_4) Such works may have been used for temple worship, court religion, ritual, instruction, or private devotion. [The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/en/exhibitions/chinese-painting-and-calligraphy-selections-from-the-collection)

When appreciating Buddhist / Daoist religious painting, do not look only at whether a "figure looks like a deity." Also observe whether the composition is symmetrical, whether the colors are solemn, how figure hierarchy is arranged, and how mudras, ritual implements, halos, and clouds organize sacred space. [The Met Journal](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Yuan_Buddhist_Mural_of_the_Paradise_of_Bhaisajyguru_The_Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_26_1991.pdf)

## Visual Identification

- **Central honored figure**: A Buddha, bodhisattva, Daoist deity, immortal, or principal figure often sits at the center, surrounded by attendants, donors, or guardians.
- **Symmetry and hierarchy**: Figure scale, position, halos, and pedestals express sacred rank.
- **Dense color**: Gold, red, blue-green, mineral pigments, and ornamental patterns increase solemnity.
- **Ritual implements and symbols**: Lotus, ruyi, scriptures, vases, swords, clouds, and halos indicate identity.
- **Ritual space**: The image resembles an altar field, pure land, palace, or celestial realm rather than ordinary perspectival realism.

## Emotion And Valence/Arousal

Buddhist / Daoist religious painting often evokes solemnity, reverence, protection, awe, compassion, or mystery. Valence is usually positive or mixed: Buddhas and bodhisattvas may feel compassionate and stable, while guardians, hell scenes, ritual images, or intense divine power can create fear and awe. Arousal is usually medium to high, depending on figure count, color density, symmetrical order, and deity posture. Symmetry reduces chaos, while dense color and many figures raise visual intensity.

In output, you might write: "The central Buddha and symmetrical attendants create stability and solemnity; the gold-red mineral colors and dense halos raise arousal; the compassionate face makes valence positive, while the guardian figures add awe and tension."

## Common Misreadings

- Do not classify all religious figure paintings as folk nianhua; Buddhist / Daoist painting is more likely to have ritual, temple, court, or devotional context and strict hierarchy.
- Do not casually assign meanings to specific deities. If the image identity is uncertain, judge cautiously from visible ritual implements, inscriptions, and object labels; inscriptions and calligraphy are important identity and context clues in Chinese painting. [Asia Society](https://asiasociety.org/education/chinese-calligraphy)
- When it overlaps with gongbi figure painting, look for stronger religious symbols, devotional function, and sacred space.
