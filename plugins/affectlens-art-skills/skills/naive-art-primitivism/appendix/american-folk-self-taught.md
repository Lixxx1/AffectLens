# American Folk / Self-Taught Adjacent Direction

American folk / self-taught adjacent work is connected to rural memory, community activity, festivals, labor, family, religion, and local stories. It is often understood within folk and self-taught art collections. Smithsonian American Art Museum's folk and self-taught art resource can be used as a reference for this collecting and interpretation framework. [Smithsonian American Art Museum](https://americanart.si.edu/blog/eye-level/2017/13/274/folk-and-self-taught-art-now-luce-center) Smithsonian's Grandma Moses resource places her in American art narrative and emphasizes that self-taught artists can form images from personal experience and community memory. [Smithsonian Institution](https://www.si.edu/stories/grandma-moses-started-painting-age-77)

When identifying it, look for scenes drawn from life memory and local tradition. Rural snow scenes, markets, harvests, churches, weddings, holidays, families, farms, and small towns often appear through flat space, repeated figures, clear outlines, and bird's-eye layouts. Smithsonian's Self-Taught Artists feature can serve as an additional reference for self-taught artists entering American art history through personal experience, materials, and visual language. [Smithsonian Institution](https://www.si.edu/spotlight/self-taughtartists) Technique may be non-academic, but the image has stable narrative order. Color is often bright; figures act directly; space resembles a memory map.

Emotionally, this direction often leans positive: nostalgia, intimacy, festivity, community, and warmth. Arousal depends on activity density: a quiet village scene is low; a market or festival is medium-high. If the image includes hard labor, religious warning, or historical trauma, valence becomes more complex. Do not read self-taught as low skill; read how personal experience and local narrative organize the image.
