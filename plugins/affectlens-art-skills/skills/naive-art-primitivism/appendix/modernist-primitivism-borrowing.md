# Modernist Primitivism Borrowing

Modernist Primitivism refers to nineteenth- and twentieth-century European modernist artists borrowing from visual sources imagined as "original," including non-Western art, folk art, child art, and early medieval art. MoMA's Primitive Art term explains that the word was once used for non-Western societies and historical European periods. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/primitive-art) At the same time, MoMA's review of the 1984 Primitivism exhibition notes that this concept drew criticism around colonial viewpoints and the erasure of context. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/interactives/moma_through_time/1980/the-infamous-primitivism-exhibition/)

When identifying it, do not call the borrowed source "primitive." Analyze how the modernist work uses mask-like faces, simplified bodies, strong outlines, non-academic proportion, flat space, or ritualized poses, and state that this is appropriation or recontextualization within European modernism rather than the Indigenous or non-Western tradition itself. MoMA's 1984 exhibition press material can be used as historical documentation for how "Primitivism in 20th Century Art" established a modernist comparison frame, while later criticism must also be considered. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/documents/moma_press-release_327377.pdf) If the work comes from Picasso, Gauguin, or similar contexts, discuss borrowing, power relations, and art-historical problems.

Emotionally, modernist Primitivism often creates intensity, roughness, mystery, bodily force, or exoticizing effects. Arousal may rise through sharp forms, mask-like faces, and strong color; valence may include vitality, anxiety, desire, or unease. Ethically, avoid using "primitive" as a praise word; make the historical problem explicit.
