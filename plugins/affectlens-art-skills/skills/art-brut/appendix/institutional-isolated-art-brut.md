# Institutional / Isolated Art Brut

## Definition and Historical Context

This direction comes from one of the most central and most ethically sensitive categories in the concept of Art Brut: works whose creators may have lived for long periods in psychiatric institutions, social isolation, private religious or cosmic systems, or outside mainstream art institutions. Collection de l'Art Brut's definition emphasizes that these works are made by self-taught makers, socially marginalized people, or psychiatric-hospital patients, often far from traditional art and trends and without a goal of public approval. [Collection de l'Art Brut](https://artbrut.ch/en/what-is-raw-art) National Galleries Scotland also notes that Dubuffet focused on works by children, visionaries, mediums, and untutored creators, but this does not mean the works can be treated as pathological specimens. [National Galleries Scotland](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/glossary-terms/art-brut)

Appreciation should focus on visible structure and creator context, not diagnosis. It is appropriate to discuss dense repetition, private numbering, fantasy worlds, and pictorial pressure, but not to say "this proves the maker had a certain illness." Art judgment should respect the maker's agency. [Collection de l'Art Brut History](https://artbrut.ch/en/history); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/art/art-brut)

## Visual Identification

- **Dense symbol systems**: Numbers, writing, eyes, faces, buildings, maps, icons, or figures appear repeatedly.
- **Private order**: The image may resemble a diary, cosmic chart, prayer system, ritual rule set, or personal historical archive.
- **Crowded composition**: The image often approaches horror vacui, with little empty space and high visual density.
- **Traces of repeated labor**: Lines, dots, grids, numbering, or coloring suggest long accumulation.
- **Closed-world feeling**: The work feels like a self-contained universe that does not necessarily explain itself to outside viewers.

## Emotion and Valence / Arousal

Arousal in this direction is usually medium-high to high. Dense repetition, sharp line, crowded space, and compulsive filling make the viewing experience tense, oppressive, absorbed, or ritualized. Valence is often mixed: bright color, symmetrical order, and decorative borders may create stability, sacredness, or hope; dark color, trapped figures, compressed space, and unreadable symbols lower valence, creating loneliness, anxiety, or unease.

In output, you can write: "The dense numbering and repeated faces raise arousal; the central symmetry creates ritual order; however, the closed space and dark background lower valence, making the emotion mixed rather than simply fearful."

## Common Misreadings

- Do not treat the work as a psychological test, and do not infer a maker's diagnosis from the image.
- Do not classify every dense pattern as Art Brut; folk ornament, religious images, and modern design can also be dense.
- Without creator context, say only that it "visually approaches Art Brut features of a private system," rather than classifying it directly.
