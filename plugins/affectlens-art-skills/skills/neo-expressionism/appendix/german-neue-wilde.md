# German Neue Wilde

German Neue Wilde is one of the most historically weighted branches of Neo-Expressionism and is often associated with Georg Baselitz, Anselm Kiefer, Markus Lupertz, A.R. Penck, and related artists. National Galleries of Scotland connects Neo-Expressionism to the 1970s-80s painting revival and mentions the German Neue Wilden as important for reintroducing rough figuration and expressive material. [National Galleries of Scotland](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/glossary-terms/neo-expressionism) Tate's Neo-Expressionism entry also provides the background of anti-minimal, anti-conceptual return to forceful painting. [Tate](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/n/neo-expressionism)

To identify it, ask whether the painting carries postwar German history, myth, national memory, or ruins. Common cues include inverted figures, rough brushwork, earth tones, black/red/yellow, wood, straw, text, hero/anti-hero imagery, ruined space, and monumental scale. The Met's Anselm Kiefer resource can serve as a reference for postwar German memory, historical burden, and heavy material language. [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/kief/hd_kief.htm) Materials may feel heavy, burned, scraped, or pressed down by history. Compared with early German Expressionism, this direction is more postmodern, more historicized, and more self-conscious about post-World War II memory.

Emotionally, Neue Wilde is often high-arousal with complex or negative valence. Impasto, rough line, huge scale, and historical symbols create anger, trauma, tragedy, resistance, or heavy reflection. Even bright color may feel satirical or uneasy. Explain how material weight, historical signs, and bodily distortion work together.
