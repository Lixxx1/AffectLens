# American Neo-Expressionism

American Neo-Expressionism is closely tied to the 1980s New York art market, street culture, consumer imagery, identity politics, material experimentation, and the return to painting. It is often associated with Jean-Michel Basquiat, Julian Schnabel, David Salle, Eric Fischl, and related artists. National Galleries of Scotland notes that the American direction absorbed American social imagery and that Schnabel's broken-plate paintings are often cited as mixed-material examples. [National Galleries of Scotland](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/glossary-terms/neo-expressionism)

To identify it, look for text, body diagrams, street signs, crowns, commodity images, collage, broken materials, and large-scale expressive painting. Basquiat-like works often contain fragmented text, anatomy, racial history, jazz, crowns, and urgent line. MoMA's Basquiat artist entry is a useful reference for his art-historical context. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/artists/370) Schnabel-like works may use broken plates, wood, fabric, and impasto to build rough surfaces; MoMA's Schnabel entry can guide mixed-material and Neo-Expressionist-adjacent context. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/artists/5257) Images often feel like a mixture of notes, walls, altars, and urban fragments.

Emotionally, the American direction is usually high-arousal. Text, signs, impasto, broken materials, and distorted figures create anger, satire, excitement, identity pressure, urban anxiety, or challenge. Valence is often complex; do not simply call it "wild." If the work uses consumer images or humor, cool irony may appear. If it involves racial violence, body, and historical trauma, the mood becomes heavier.
