# Other International Adjacent Directions

Other international adjacent directions include Spanish, French, British, Latin American, and other regional returns to figuration, gesture, history, myth, and painterly material around the 1970s and 1980s. Tate and National Galleries of Scotland definitions of Neo-Expressionism can serve as broad references for this return to painting, forceful figuration, and anti-cool formal language. [Tate](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/n/neo-expressionism) [National Galleries of Scotland](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/glossary-terms/neo-expressionism) These works may not strictly belong to Neo-Expressionism, but they share the international "new painting" atmosphere. Distinguish local context and avoid classifying all rough figurative painting as Neo-Expressionism.

To identify this adjacency, look for large scale, strong gesture, rough materials, mythic/historical symbols, violent bodies, image collage, and reaction against Minimalist or Conceptual coolness. National Gallery of Art's *Teaching Art Since 1950* can serve as a teaching reference for the coexistence of abstraction, conceptual art, return to painting, and diverse local contexts in postwar contemporary art. [National Gallery of Art](https://www.nga.gov/learn/teachers/lessons-activities/contemporary-art.html) Different regions may add local politics, religion, colonial memory, urban culture, or personal mythology. If the work's date or regional context is clear, prefer the more specific local movement name.

Emotionally, these works usually have medium-high to high arousal and complex valence. Rough painterly action, image collision, and historical signs can create unease, frenzy, anger, humor, or tragedy. Use "Neo-Expressionist-adjacent" cautiously and state the evidence and uncertainty.
