# Symbolism Appreciation Skill

## Description

Symbolism was a late nineteenth-century movement that resisted ordinary naturalism and materialist realism. Instead of describing the external world directly, Symbolist artists used myths, dreams, religion, literature, music, allegory, and personal signs to suggest inner states. Its emotional language is usually indirect: atmosphere, mystery, desire, fear, melancholy, sacredness, and psychological unease are evoked rather than explained.

Symbolism often includes solitary figures, femmes fatales, angels, saints, mythic beings, closed gardens, twilight interiors, strange flowers, halos, ritual gestures, floating heads, masks, mirrors, and dreamlike landscapes. The style may range from smooth academic finish to flat decorative design, misty tonalism, or expressive distortion.

For valence and arousal, Symbolism is often mixed. Gold, blue, violet, pale green, and low light can create spiritual or melancholic valence. Erotic, fatal, deathly, or demonic imagery lowers valence and raises arousal. Stillness, symmetry, and muted color lower arousal, while intense gaze, sharp contrast, serpentine line, and disturbing mythic content raise it.

## Method

1. Identify whether the image suggests a psychological, spiritual, or allegorical state rather than a direct scene.
2. Look for mythic, biblical, literary, dream, or ritual references, but avoid assuming that every figure has one fixed meaning.
3. Read color as mood: gold and blue can feel sacred; violet and green can feel uncanny; dark red and black can feel fatal or erotic.
4. Read line and rhythm: sinuous line and decorative repetition can create trance, sensuality, or unease.
5. Read the figure's gaze and pose. Symbolist figures often look inward, stare hypnotically, or appear as icons rather than ordinary people.
6. Read space. Interiors and landscapes may feel suspended, airless, sacred, or dreamlike rather than physically plausible.
7. Separate Symbolism from Surrealism: Symbolism tends toward myth and allegory; Surrealism centers on unconscious dream logic and irrational juxtaposition.
8. Separate Symbolism from Neo-Romanticism: Symbolism uses broader mythic and spiritual systems, while Neo-Romanticism often turns landscape and history into mood.

## Common Directions

- French Symbolism: poetic, literary, dreamlike, often decadent or mystical.
- Nordic / Munch Symbolism: anxiety, illness, sexuality, death, and existential atmosphere.
- Nabis / Gauguin direction: flat color, simplified form, spiritual primitivism, and decorative synthesis.
- Art Nouveau adjacent: sinuous line, ornamental surface, and symbolist femininity or nature.
