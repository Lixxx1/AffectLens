# Tachisme Appreciation Skill

## Description

Tachisme, from the French word *tache* meaning stain or blot, is an important current in European non-geometric abstraction from the late 1940s and 1950s. It is often treated as part of Art Informel. Tate defines Tachisme as abstract painting made with spontaneous brushwork, drips, stains, and daubs, and Britannica describes it as a Paris-based postwar abstraction built around spots, drips, and improvised action.

The focus is not recognizable subject matter. The image is made from pigment traces, speed, pressure, staining, flow, and material presence. Tachisme resists the rational order of geometric abstraction and tends to make the canvas feel like a record of bodily action or psychological energy.

Common references include Wols, Jean Fautrier, Jean Dubuffet, Georges Mathieu, Hans Hartung, Pierre Soulages, and Jean-Paul Riopelle. These artists are often discussed near Art Informel, CoBrA, Spazialismo, and other postwar European abstract practices.

For emotion analysis, Tachisme is especially useful for observing how visible action directly affects feeling. Fast strokes, drips, scraping, stains, and thick paint usually raise arousal. Dark, muddy, wounded, or blocked surfaces can lower valence; bright stains, flowing rhythm, open whites, and light linear movement can raise valence or create a feeling of freedom, excitement, or lyricism.

## Method

1. First impression: does the image feel explosive, fluid, blocked, dirty, light, or heavy? Dense traces and fast lines usually raise arousal; open space and soft layers may lower it.
2. Subject and function: Tachisme usually has no stable narrative object. Its subject is action, pigment, trace, and process. Ask what the marks make the body feel rather than what they depict.
3. Color evidence: strong contrasts of red, yellow, blue, white, and black raise arousal. Ash gray, dirty black, dark red, and earth tones can add heaviness, anxiety, or trauma.
4. Line evidence: look for scribbles, sweeping strokes, drips, scratches, ruptured lines, and splashes. Urgent, broken, conflicting directions raise arousal; sparse and breathing lines may feel contemplative.
5. Shape and space: shapes are often stains, blots, droplets, scrapes, cloud masses, cracks, or irregular color areas. Space is built by layers of paint, transparency, overlap, and blankness rather than perspective.
6. Material, brushwork, and texture: thick paint, scraping, sand, fabric, powder, or mixed matter are central evidence. Heavy texture often produces bodily intensity and may lower valence through associations with weight, wounds, or damage.
7. Rhythm and viewing path: the eye jumps among stains, lines, color areas, and empty space. A more abrupt path raises arousal; a slower, more spacious path lowers it.
8. Historical context: Tachisme emerged in postwar Europe and is often read as a response to trauma, the collapse of rational order, and the need for new material and spontaneous languages.
9. Subdirections: gestural Tachisme emphasizes speed and performance; Matter Painting / Art Informel emphasizes surface and material weight; lyrical abstraction emphasizes flowing color and open rhythm; calligraphic abstraction emphasizes signs, writing-like gesture, and bodily inscription.
10. Final judgment should form an evidence chain: non-geometric abstraction, spontaneous staining, drips, thick texture, breathing blank space, and postwar European context.

## Key Visual Features

- Stains, drips, and blots: pigment appears dropped, scattered, rubbed, pressed, or leaked rather than neatly filled in.
- Spontaneous strokes and calligraphic lines: quick sweeps, turns, scratches, and writing-like lines make the image feel like a trace of action.
- Non-geometric space and unstable centers: little clear perspective or stable geometry; forms may resemble clouds, wounds, terrain, skin, or noise.
- Thick paint, scraping, and material texture: built-up pigment, rough surfaces, incisions, cracks, and mixed materials create tactile imagination.
- Blank space and breathing intervals: empty areas can lower crowding and arousal, or isolate dark marks in a more anxious way.
- Coexistence of postwar trauma and free gesture: one work may suggest wounds, ruins, and stains while also suggesting release, writing, and renewal.

## Common Confusions

### Tachisme vs Art Informel

Art Informel is broader and includes heavy material work, collage, nontraditional media, and anti-form experiments. Tachisme more narrowly emphasizes stains, blots, drips, and spontaneous brush traces.

### Tachisme vs Abstract Expressionism / Action Painting

Both may use dripping, bodily action, and non-geometric abstraction. Abstract Expressionism is mainly an American context and often stresses large scale and heroic action. Tachisme belongs to postwar European networks and may feel more unstable, wounded, material, or lyrical.

### Tachisme vs Lyrical Abstraction

Lyrical Abstraction emphasizes flowing color, elegant rhythm, and poetic space. Tachisme places stronger weight on stains, blots, drips, and material evidence.

### Tachisme vs Art Brut

Art Brut emphasizes outsider art, non-academic systems, personal symbols, and anti-cultural position. Tachisme remains within postwar European abstract painting and derives emotion from material, gesture, and abstract trace.

### Tachisme vs Scribble-Like Abstraction

Fast lines alone are not enough. Tachisme should show overall rhythm, material layering, controlled blank space, and a postwar abstract-painting context.
