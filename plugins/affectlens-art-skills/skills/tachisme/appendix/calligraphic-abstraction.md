# Calligraphic Abstraction

## Definition and Context

Calligraphic abstraction brings signs, cursive movement, speed, and bodily writing into the non-geometric field of Tachisme / Art Informel. Artists such as Georges Mathieu are useful references for speed, sign-like marks, and public performative gesture in postwar European abstraction.

The marks do not need to be readable writing. Writing-like action becomes visual trace.

## Image Identification

Look for lines that resemble characters, signs, cursive marks, signatures, scratches, or quick brush gestures. They should feel like traces left by the body rather than outlines of objects.

The image may include long lines, short abrupt strokes, ink-like marks, splashes, sudden directional changes, and rhythmic blank space. Color may be simple or intense; the key is rhythm and gesture, not literal reading.

## Emotion and Valence/Arousal

Arousal depends on line tension. Long fluent strokes may feel releasing and elegant, with medium arousal. Sharp, broken, dense, or high-contrast strokes raise tension. Valence can feel heroic, ecstatic, anxious, or ceremonial depending on speed, force, and spacing.

Explain that the line seems to write rather than depict, and identify speed, pressure, and reserve space.

## Misclassification Warning

This direction can be confused with East Asian calligraphy, scribble drawing, and sign painting. If the work uses calligraphic language within postwar European abstraction and emphasizes improvisation and non-geometric abstraction, it can be treated here. If the text is readable and serves linguistic content, it may be text art. If the marks mainly come from street culture, it may be graffiti-like abstraction.
