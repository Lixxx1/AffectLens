# Bay Area Figurative First Generation

The first generation of Bay Area Figuration usually refers to artists such as David Park, Elmer Bischoff, and Richard Diebenkorn, who turned from Abstract Expressionism back toward figures, landscapes, and everyday scenes in the San Francisco Bay Area during the 1950s. The Art Story's overview of the Bay Area Figurative Movement is a useful reference for this group and historical turn. This was not a simple return to realism. It brought broad brushwork, color-plane structure, and bodily painting energy from Abstract Expressionism back into recognizable people and spaces. SFMOMA's material on David Park emphasizes that he abandoned abstraction and began making "pictures," a key starting point for Bay Area Figurative Art.

## Recognition

Look for figures and scenes reappearing inside abstract color planes. Beaches, pools, interiors, musicians, seated figures, landscapes, and everyday gatherings may be handled as large color blocks, thick edges, and loose contours. Figures remain recognizable but are not finely realist; space remains readable but may be flattened by color. Diebenkorn-like works especially move back and forth between figuration and abstraction: people, windows, tables, or landscapes are both real scenes and compositional structures.

## Emotion and Valence/Arousal

First-generation Bay Area Figuration often moves between warmth, introspection, intimacy, and slight estrangement. Bright California light, open color blocks, and everyday subjects can make valence positive; heavy brushwork, silent figures, spatial separation, and cold gray-blue tones make valence complex. Arousal is usually medium: livelier than traditional realism, but less aggressive than Neo-expressionism. If figures are pressed by color planes or do not interact, loneliness may emerge.

## Common Confusion

It can be confused with Abstract Expressionism or ordinary figurative painting. If there are no recognizable figures or scenes, it may be Abstract Expressionism. If figures are realist, brushwork hidden, and space stable, it is closer to realism. The key is the coexistence of recognizable daily imagery and Abstract Expressionist painting behavior. In analysis, describe both figure posture and color/brushwork; do not simply say "roughly painted."
