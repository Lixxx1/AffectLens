# Bay Area Figurative Second Generation and Continuation

The second generation and continuation of Bay Area Figuration often includes artists such as Joan Brown, Nathan Oliveira, Manuel Neri, Paul Wonner, and William Theophilus Brown. The Art Story's Bay Area Figurative Movement overview provides a reference for the relationship between the first generation and later artists. This direction continues the move from Abstract Expressionism back toward figuration, but it may emphasize body psychology, personal narrative, sculptural presence, isolated figures, or rougher surfaces. Crocker Art Museum notes that a new generation of Bay Area artists brought Abstract Expressionist self-reference into figures, nudes, studios, and California landscapes.

## Recognition

Look for figures that feel more personal and psychologically charged. Joan Brown may feel flatter, more narrative, and more autobiographical; Nathan Oliveira's bodies often seem isolated, indistinct, and emerging from empty grounds; Manuel Neri's sculptural bodies emphasize volume, surface, and posture. Compared with the first generation, this direction may focus less on group scenes or open California light and more on individual figures, bodily weight, psychological distance, and material handling.

## Emotion and Valence/Arousal

Valence is often more complex. Everyday subjects may feel melancholy, reflective, anxious, or fragile because figures are isolated, backgrounds empty, bodies unfinished, or color heavy. Bright color and humorous narrative may create lightness, satire, or intimacy. Arousal depends on material force: thick paint, rough surfaces, and exaggerated posture raise arousal; empty backgrounds, soft contour, and low saturation lower it while increasing loneliness or introspection.

## Common Confusion

This direction can be confused with Neo-expressionism or contemporary psychological portraiture. Neo-expressionism is usually later, larger, more historical or political, and more aggressive in brushwork. Bay Area second-generation work more often emerges from California art contexts, studio figures, and personal narrative. If the source is unclear, use "expressive figurative tendency" rather than forcing the Bay Area label.
