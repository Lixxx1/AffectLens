# New Figuration-Adjacent Direction

New Figuration-adjacent work refers to postwar European, Latin American, and related contexts in which artists returned to the figure and recognizable imagery against impersonal abstraction. Tate defines New Figuration as a postwar tendency that renewed attention to figures, bodies, and recognizable images. Like Bay Area Figuration, it returns to image after abstraction; unlike Bay Area Figuration, it more often carries social, political, existential, grotesque, or historical critique, especially in European and Latin American contexts.

## Recognition

Look for whether the figure carries pressure from its time. The image may include distorted bodies, theatrical postures, rough brushwork, grotesque faces, political implication, urban violence, identity anxiety, or social unease. Tate's Figurative Art entry can serve as a basic reference for works centered on recognizable people or objects. Compared with Bay Area scenes of beaches, interiors, or gatherings, New Figuration-adjacent work more often turns the body into a carrier of historical or social conflict.

## Emotion and Valence/Arousal

Valence often tends negative or complex, and arousal is usually medium-high to high. Rough brushwork, intense bodily distortion, piercing color, and social symbols can create tension, anger, unease, satire, or trauma. If figures are handled through humor, comics, or popular imagery, valence may become satirically mixed. Ask why the figure is unstable: is it formal experiment, or social, political, or existential anxiety?

## Common Confusion

This direction can be confused with Neo-expressionism or Social Realism. Tate's Neo-expressionism entry helps distinguish the 1980s painting revival and market/postmodern context. Social Realism depicts labor, poverty, class, or political stance more directly. New Figuration-adjacent work sits between them: it returns to the figure, but is not necessarily propaganda realism and not necessarily 1980s Neo-expressionism. If provenance is unclear, describe it cautiously as "a postwar figurative-expressionist tendency close to New Figuration."
