# Korean Buddhist Painting

Korean Buddhist painting traces back to Goryeo and Joseon religious image traditions. Subjects include Buddhas, bodhisattvas, arhats, Ksitigarbha, Avalokitesvara, Water Moon Avalokitesvara, the Ten Kings, Pure Land imagery, and temple ritual images. The National Museum of Korea's Buddhist Paintings Room can serve as a reference for subjects, offering functions, and temple image traditions. [National Museum of Korea](https://www.museum.go.kr/site/eng/showroom/758/view) These works usually have religious functions and are not merely decorative paintings. The museum's Water Moon Avalokitesvara example is useful for solemn composition, devotional function, and refined color. [National Museum of Korea](https://www.museum.go.kr/ENG/contents/E0402000000.do?relicId=7953&schM=view&searchId=search)

To identify it, ask whether the composition is symmetrical, centered, and ritualized. Buddhas or bodhisattvas often occupy the center, surrounded by halos, lotus thrones, attendants, clouds, donors, or ornamental patterns. The Met Libraries' *Arts of Korea* can serve as broader art-historical context for the coexistence of Buddhist, court, and folk art in Korea. [The Metropolitan Museum of Art Libraries](https://libmma.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/collection/p15324coll10/id/46962) Color may use gold, red, green, and blue, with fine line and solemn figures. Compared with literati ink painting, Buddhist painting emphasizes iconographic order, religious identity, and devotional function. Compared with minhwa, it is more solemn, hierarchical, and ritualized.

Emotionally, Korean Buddhist painting often feels solemn, sacred, compassionate, reverent, or awe-filled. Gold-blue color, symmetrical composition, frontal gaze, and dense pattern raise arousal to medium or medium-high, while the stable center and religious order prevent chaos. Valence is often positive/solemn mixed: viewers may feel peace and reverence, but darker colors, dense deities, or underworld themes can add heaviness. Distinguish the different emotional tone of compassionate Avalokitesvara, Pure Land imagery, and Ten Kings or underworld subjects.

It is easily confused with Chinese or Japanese Buddhist painting. Korean Buddhist art shares East Asian Buddhist iconography, so materials, facial style, color, clothing, inscription, provenance, and collection context should guide judgment. If origin is unclear, describe it cautiously as East Asian Buddhist painting and state whether Korean evidence is present.
