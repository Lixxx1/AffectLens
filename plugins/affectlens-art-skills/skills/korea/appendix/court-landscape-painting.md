# Court Landscape Painting

Joseon court landscape painting was influenced by Chinese Song, Yuan, and Ming painting, but gradually developed a steadier, clearer, more lyrical, and more restrained Korean vocabulary. The Met's Korean landscape resource explains how Joseon landscape painting from 1400 to 1800 absorbed Chinese styles, developed court painting institutions, and became Koreanized through true-view landscape by artists such as Jeong Seon. [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/mountain-and-water-korean-landscape-painting-1400-1800) Court landscapes often use ink as the main medium with pale color, multiple viewpoints, empty space, and inscriptions to create an open, quiet viewing experience.

To identify it, ask whether the landscape has dignified structure and gentle breath. Mountains, pines, streams, pavilions, and small figures may be arranged along a relaxed path, with the gaze moving through mountain roads, mist, and water. The Met's *Art of the Korean Renaissance* can serve as context for the formation of early Joseon court, literati, and painting traditions. [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2009/art-of-the-korean-renaissance) Color is usually restrained: pale blue-green, light ocher, and ink create quiet layers. True-view landscape may depict specific Korean sites such as the Diamond Mountains, adding travel, memory, and local identity to the landscape.

Emotionally, court landscape usually has low to medium arousal and positive or gently complex valence. Large empty spaces, pale ink, and steady line bring calm, elegance, openness, and introspection. When mountain forms are steep, ink is heavy, or paths turn sharply, arousal rises and the mood can become sublime or coolly austere. Korean landscape melancholy is not simply negative sadness; it can be nostalgia, distance, and restrained feeling within calm.

It is easily confused with Chinese landscape and Japanese ink painting. Joseon landscape is indeed influenced by China, but it often feels more restrained, gentle, stable, and attentive to empty space and natural harmony. The National Museum of Korea's Joseon Dynasty Gallery can provide context for Joseon painting and material culture. [National Museum of Korea](https://www.museum.go.kr/site/eng/showroom/757/view) Japanese ink painting may be more Zen-like, minimal, or decorative in some cases. Judge by inscription, hanji texture, Korean place names, court context, and the overall mild temperament.
