# Decollage Posters

## Definition And Context

In Nouveau Realisme, decollage refers to tearing down, peeling, and re-presenting street posters, advertising paper, political propaganda, and urban wall fragments. National Galleries of Scotland's Decollage entry can be used as a definition reference for torn or peeled poster imagery. [National Galleries of Scotland](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/glossary-terms/decollage) It is associated with Raymond Hains, Jacques Villegle, Francois Dufrene, and Mimmo Rotella. Unlike collage, which adds material, decollage removes and tears: the image comes from the damaged remains of an urban surface.

## Visual Identification

Look for paper layers and edges: overlapping posters, broken letters, missing figures or products, ragged torn borders, exposed old paper, glue traces, wall surface, or earlier printed layers. Tate's Nouveau realisme resource can serve as a movement context for real urban materials, consumer images, and public-space fragments entering art. [Tate](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/n/nouveau-realisme) Text is often incomplete, as if city information has been interrupted by noise. Color may be bright, but it is damaged by grime, folds, tearing, and dullness. If the image looks abstract but contains recognizable advertising type, political slogans, or street-print color blocks, consider decollage.

## Emotion: Valence / Arousal

Decollage usually produces medium-high arousal because tearing, broken text, color conflict, and layered fragments create visual noise. Valence is often mixed or negative: damage and urban decay lower valence, while bright advertising color can add ironic vitality. Political or consumer content adds public-life tension. Explain how torn edges, broken text, paper thickness, and wall remnants drive emotion.

## Common Misreadings

Decollage and Pop Art can both involve advertising. Pop Art often reproduces or redraws commercial signs with cleaner surfaces; decollage uses real street-poster fragments and emphasizes damage, removal, and public-space traces. Centre Pompidou's New Realism resource can serve as a boundary reference for real fragments, urban materials, and consumer-society surfaces. [Centre Pompidou](https://mediation.centrepompidou.fr/education/ressources/ENS-newrea-EN/ENS-newrea-EN.htm) If a work only cuts and pastes newspaper without peeling, tearing, or street-poster context, it is more likely collage.
