# Postwar / Neo-Expressionist-Adjacent Direction

The postwar / Neo-expressionist-adjacent direction refers to the revival and reworking of Expressionist language from the 1970s and 1980s onward, often called Neo-expressionism. Tate and National Galleries of Scotland both frame Neo-expressionism as a reaction against Minimalism, Conceptual Art, and cool formal restraint. [Tate](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/n/neo-expressionism) [National Galleries of Scotland](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/glossary-terms/neo-expressionism) It is not early German Expressionism itself, but it clearly inherits strong color, rough brushwork, distorted figures, historical trauma, political imagery, and large-scale painting.

When identifying it, ask whether the painting feels larger, thicker, and more postmodern than early Expressionism. Common features include huge canvases, heavy pigment buildup, violent brushwork, scrawled text, historical symbols, mythic figures, political suggestion, broken bodies, and collage-like image sources. Sotheby's overview of Neo-Expressionism can be used as supplemental reference for large scale, intense figuration, and the 1970s-80s revival context. [Sotheby's](https://www.sothebys.com/en/art-movements/neo-expressionism) Early Die Brucke often centers urban and bodily anxiety; Der Blaue Reiter centers spiritual abstraction; Neo-expressionism may mix history, media, memory, identity, and the act of painting. It often looks rougher, more theatrical, and more aware that it is reviving expressive painting.

Emotionally, Neo-expressionism is often very high in arousal. Huge scale, impasto, strong color, figure distortion, and historical symbols create anger, trauma, provocation, absurdity, tragedy, or frenzy. Valence is often negative or complex, though the painting energy itself may produce excitement and release. Compared with Abstract Expressionism, it is usually more figurative, image-based, and historical. Compared with early Expressionism, its context comes more from after World War II, the Cold War, media culture, and the postmodern art market.

The main confusions are Abstract Expressionism and Figurative Expressionism. Abstract Expressionism may have equally strong gesture and material, but it usually lacks obvious figures, historical imagery, or narrative signs. Figurative Expressionism is broader and does not necessarily carry the 1970s-80s Neo-expressionist revival context. Do not classify only from wild brushwork; ask whether the work is reusing Expressionist language within a postwar or postmodern historical setting.
