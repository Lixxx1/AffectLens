# American Neo-Pop

American Neo-Pop continues Pop Art's interest in consumer goods, celebrities, advertising, toys, and mass culture, but after the 1980s it is closely tied to postmodern art markets, industrial fabrication, luxury goods, commercial gloss, and branded viewing. Art Gallery of New South Wales material on *Pop, Neo-Pop, Post-Pop* can serve as a reference for the continuum from Pop to Neo-Pop/Post-Pop. Jeff Koons, Keith Haring, Kenny Scharf, and Damien Hirst are often placed in Neo-Pop or post-Pop contexts.

## Recognition

Look for objects from consumption and entertainment: balloon dogs, toys, cartoon figures, celebrities, household goods, hearts, shiny metal, polished plastic, luxury packaging, and enlarged commodities. The Met's *Jeff Koons on the Roof* can serve as a reference for enlarged scale, glossy surface, and consumer/entertainment objects entering museum space. Surfaces are often highly finished, industrial, bright, and glossy. Emotion moves between pleasure and satire. Compared with 1960s Pop Art, Neo-Pop is often more self-aware about the art market, branding, and commodity worship.

## Emotion and Valence/Arousal

American Neo-Pop often has positive surface valence and medium-high arousal: bright color, large scale, and shiny surfaces create excitement, pleasure, and spectacle. Taubman Museum's *POP Power from Warhol to Koons* can be used as a reference for mass culture, consumer imagery, and art-market attraction from Warhol to Koons. Yet excessive perfection and consumer worship can also create emptiness, coldness, or irony. In analysis, write both the attraction and the critique/ambiguity.
