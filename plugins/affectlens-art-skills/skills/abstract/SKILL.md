# Abstract Art Appreciation Skill

## describe

Abstract Art is a type of art that no longer attempts to reproduce the external scene. It usually replaces the direct depiction of reality with shapes, colors, forms and gestures [^30]. The artist creates a new visual language through shapes, colors, lines and textures, focusing on exploring inner emotions and spiritual experiences. Kandinsky believed that colors, lines and shapes can form a "visual symphony" that directly evokes the emotions and soul of the viewer: he pointed out in "On Spirit in Art" that color can cause psychological and physical reactions, yellow is disturbing, and blue evokes the highest spiritual yearning[^1]. The Cleveland Museum of Art pointed out that Kandinsky regarded music as the perfect abstract art, which could evoke strong emotions without words or narratives; he hoped that painting, like music, could reach people's subconscious [^3] through abstract forms. Therefore, abstract art is often associated with musical rhythm, harmony and emotion, and the colors and lines of the picture are often as "audible" as melody and rhythm.

New research and museum education resources emphasize that abstract art uses the elements themselves to express ideas and emotions. The syllabus of the National Museum of Women in the Arts (NMWA) points out that the subject of abstract art is not characters or objects, but the arrangement of colors, lines, shapes, spaces, and textures; students should realize that abstract art often uses simplified or distorted shapes, exaggerated colors, and flattened spaces to express ideas and emotions [^4]. The University of Utah Art Museum's "Emotional Orchestration" course also emphasizes that colors and shapes themselves have the same power to express emotions as familiar images, and students explore whether different colors and shapes are associated with certain emotions[^6]. When appreciating this type of work, viewers can take the initiative to ask: "What colors do I see? Which colors are the most? What do these colors remind me of? What shapes are there, and do they repeat? Are they geometric or organic? What lines do I notice, and what are their directions? Are there textures or repeating patterns?" This process of observation and reflection helps us understand the emotions that the artist may want to convey [^4]. The Canton Museum of Art explains that abstract art has no fixed meaning, and viewers should respond to rhythm, contrast, or atmosphere with their personal experiences; emotion, spirituality, or formal experimentation may be the purpose of creation, and the reaction itself is part of the meaning of the work [^8].

This skill is useful for identifying, understanding, and comparing abstract works of art, especially when the user wishes to analyze the emotional impact of the painting. By learning how to analyze color, line, shape, space, and materials in abstract paintings, even viewers with no previous exposure to this type of art can gradually interpret the work and infer the emotions it may evoke.

## Appreciation method

1. Identify the subject matter and look and feel: Abstract art often has no specific subject matter or only implies natural forms. Start by feeling whether the work is completely non-representational, and record the emotions you feel when you first look at it (such as tranquility, tension, joy, or confusion).

2. Color evidence: Observe in detail the three major characteristics of color: hue (red, blue, etc.), value (bright or dark), and purity (bright or dull). Getty formal analysis data also uses these attributes as the basis for understanding the emotional role of color [^9]. Art education materials point out that changes in brightness can create different atmospheres: dark colors can suggest night scenes or dark environments, creating mysterious or uneasy emotions; light colors can suggest light sources, often with emotions of tranquility or hope[^9]. Color purity also affects mood: bright colors are often associated with positive energy and high arousal, while mixed and harmonious colors convey a solemn or calm atmosphere[^9]. Kandinsky emphasized that different colors evoke different psychological feelings. Yellow is disturbing, while blue brings spiritual yearning[^1]. Also pay attention to the ratio of warm colors (reds, oranges, yellows) to cool colors (blues, greens, grays): warm color blocks and strong contrasts often enhance arousal, while cool colors and soft gradients lend themselves to contemplation. Record the main hues, color area and arrangement, and determine how they affect the valence and arousal of the mood. When choosing colors, consider color symbolism and personal associations. The educational campaign "Emotion Abstract Art" advises students to use color, shape and texture to convey different emotions and encourages them to create color palettes [^11] for emotions such as happiness, anger or sadness. So think of red as often associated with passion or anger, blue as conveying tranquility or sadness, yellow as expressing joy or hope, etc. Also, pay attention to the rhythm and gradients between colors: continuous stripes or gradients, like rhythmic changes in music, will enhance the fluidity of emotion.

3. Line evidence: Analyze the type and direction of lines. Getty’s formal analysis of lines can be used as the basis for the emotional effects of horizontal, vertical, grid, diagonal and curved lines. Refer to [^9]. Educational resources point out that horizontal lines, parallel to the ground, are often associated with rest and tranquility; they also help differentiate the layers of a picture and create a sense of space[^9]. The vertical lines extend upward, making people feel height and spirituality, bringing solemn or transcendent emotions [^9]. Interlaced horizontal and vertical straight lines (such as a grid) convey stability and reliability, reducing tension [^9]. On the contrary, slashes express movement or instability, creating a dynamic and tense atmosphere[^9]. The curved lines are soft and full of vitality, with sensual, smooth and sensual qualities [^9]. Observe the thickness, continuity, and rhythm of the lines, judging how they guide your eye, whether they move steadily or jump suddenly, and think about how this rhythm affects the arousal. Thick or rough handwriting may bring a sense of strength and arousal, while fine, even lines create a sense of peace. The direction and arrangement of lines not only affect rhythm, but also imply emotional directionality. The Museum of Western American Art discussed Emil Bystram's "Syncopated Rhythm." The artist pointed out that when the picture moves from the lower left to the upper right, it shows joy and positive emotions; on the contrary, if it moves from the upper left to the lower right, it will appear sad or dramatic [^18]. Observe whether your eyes move in a certain direction, whether there is a strong upward or downward tendency. This "reading path" will affect the emotional tendency. Also pay attention to the correspondence between line rhythm and music: continuous parallel lines are like gentle melody, broken or intersecting lines are like sudden beat hits [^18].

4. Shape/spatial evidence: Identify the types and spatial relationships of shapes. Geometric shapes (such as squares, circles, triangles) are regular and artificial, making the picture appear rational and stable[^9]; organic shapes are irregular, imitating nature, and often have a sense of life and uncertainty[^9]. The proportion of geometric and organic forms in the painting affects the proportion of rationality or emotion you experience. Pay attention to both positive and negative space: the area occupied by the main shape is positive space, and the surrounding blank space is negative space. The presence of a large amount of negative space can make the subject more isolated or quiet, while the compact positive space increases the sense of oppression; Getty's explanation of the relationship between shape and space can be used to support this type of judgment [^9]. Observe whether the shape arrangement is symmetrical or free, and whether it creates a visual rhythm: symmetrical and repeated shapes bring security and order; randomness and overlap may cause uneasiness or excitement. Flattening or changing the depth of space also affects arousal: flattening space focuses the eye more on surface forms, while depth and perspective create extensions of space, guiding the eye deeper. Shapes can also be given personal symbolic meaning. The Detroit Institute of Arts' "Breaking with Tradition" educational resource encourages students to create their own "symbol library" using abstract lines and shapes to represent identity or memory, such as zigzag lines to symbolize the strength to overcome challenges, spirals to symbolize growth, repeating dots to represent family or community, and gentle waves to represent peace or hope [^23]. As you look at the work, you can observe whether the artist used repeated symbols or patterns, which may represent personal, cultural or social meaning. The event "Abstract Memories" at the Georgia O'Keeffe Museum emphasizes that abstract art uses color, line and shape to make the picture "feel" a specific emotion, and it does not have to look like any real object[^24]; viewers can also try to map their own experiences into the shape combination of the picture.

5. Composition/material evidence: Analyze whether the overall composition is concentrated in the center or spread across the entire frame. Are the elements in the composition aligned to create order, or are they scattered freely to create a sense of instability? Large paintings surround the viewer, increasing immersion; smaller works provide an intimate experience. The material also determines the visual weight: smooth and flat surfaces and uniform tones tend to be calming and support low arousal; the texture of impasto, drip painting or collage emphasizes the material itself, increases physicality and materiality, and makes the mood more tense. Note whether traditional media (paint, acrylic) or non-traditional materials (sand, wood) are used and how these materials enhance visual and tactile sensations. Composition also includes negative space and contrast with texture. The Museum of Western Art in the United States analyzed John Marin's work and pointed out that he juxtaposed smooth colors with rough brushstrokes of dry brushing in the painting, and used dissonant color combinations such as mustard yellow and blue-green, indigo and tan to create friction, leaving uncolored paper as negative space [^18]. This contrast can be like "harmony and dissonance" in music, both intensifying energy and providing breath. Observe whether there are layers of paint or transparent white space in the painting, and how they create visual rhythm and fluctuations. Also pay attention to the physical direction of the picture: when the painting moves from the bottom left to the top right, it will give people a light feeling, and vice versa will produce a feeling of depression[^18].

6. Historical information and genre breakdown: Understanding the creative background helps interpret emotions. For example, geometric abstraction and neoplasticism pursue rational order, and the pictures form a "visual language" through vertical and horizontal squares, primary colors, and black and white. Mondrian completely abandoned natural images in "Composition of Red, Yellow and Blue" and only used grids and three primary colors, hoping to create a language that could convey universal harmony after the war [^26]. Therefore, such works tend to give people a stable and balanced low arousal feeling. On the contrary, lyrical abstraction or expressive abstraction pays more attention to personal emotions and improvisational expression. The pictures are free and unrestrained, and the mood fluctuates greatly. Consider whether the work is a rational pursuit, a spiritual exploration, or a reflection of social turmoil based on the artist's life and the ideological trends of the times. Additionally, many abstract artists are inspired by music, philosophy, or science and attempt to evoke emotions through rhythm, repetition, and spacing, much like music does. The Canton Museum of Art emphasizes that abstract art invites personal interpretation and has no fixed meaning; many artists believe that color can express sound and emotion like music[^8]. The Museum of Western Art pointed out that modernist artists would listen to historical and contemporary music to obtain harmonious and rhythmic inspiration. Music can tell stories just like paintings [^18]. Understanding these cross-media inspirations helps us appreciate the rhythm and emotional source of the work.

7. Comprehensive judgment: Based on the above evidence of color, line, shape space and compositional materials, judge the main emotions that the work may evoke, and explain which evidence determines valence and which evidence affects arousal. If some features overlap with other genres, point out any uncertainties.

## Key visual features

- Non-representational composition and reading path: The work uses geometric or organic shapes to form a new order, without traditional perspective and focus. A strict grid of vertical and horizontal lines (such as Mondrian's "Composition of Red, Yellow and Blue" [^26]) divides the picture into stable rectangles, guiding the line of sight to move slowly, creating a calm and rational mood; free and irregular shapes, diagonal lines and scattered dots make the line of sight jump constantly, increasing dynamics and uncertainty.

- Reading path and directionality: The reading path of the work will affect the mood. The discussion of "Syncopated Rhythm" by the Museum of Western Art pointed out that when the picture moves from the lower left to the upper right, it shows a light and positive mood, while when it moves from the upper left to the lower right, it appears dramatic or sad [^18]. Observe whether the composition has a clear direction, whether the lines lift upwards or fall downwards, move horizontally or follow a diagonal rhythm, and determine how this directionality shapes the mood of the work.

- Pure use of color and its meaning: Primary colors or limited palettes are commonly used in abstract art. Color has three elements: hue, brightness and purity[^9]. Dark colors and low lightness create mysterious or heavy emotions, while light colors and high lightness create light and tranquility[^9]; bright colors with high purity are usually associated with positive energy and high arousal, and mixed or dark tones create a solemn or contemplative atmosphere[^9]. Warm colors and strong contrast enhance arousal, cool colors and gradients reduce arousal. Kandinsky believed that yellow was disturbing and blue evoked spiritual yearning [^1].

- The emotional rhythm of lines and brushstrokes: the direction of the lines determines the emotion. Horizontal lines symbolize rest and tranquility, and help distinguish the layers of the picture [^9]; vertical lines extend upward, bringing height and spirituality [^9]; horizontal and vertical grids convey stability and reliability [^9]; diagonal lines imply movement and instability [^9]; curved lines are soft and rich in sensual qualities [^9]. The strength and speed of brush strokes also affect rhythm: thick or free-flowing strokes increase tension and high arousal; fine, even lines create calmness and low arousal.

- Music and Rhythm Metaphors: Many abstract artists draw on music to construct rhythm. The Cleveland Museum of Art pointed out that Kandinsky wanted painting to directly touch the viewer's subconscious [^3] through abstract form like music. The Museum of Western Art further explains that modernist painters often listened to music to get inspiration for harmony and rhythm [^18]. Stripe painter Gene Davis believes that colors, lines and geometric shapes have the same expressive power as figurative works[^6]; viewers can feel the color bands, repeated lines and rhythm changes like listening to music, and experience the "melody" and "beat" of the picture.

- The psychological weight of texture and materials: Smooth and flat surfaces and uniform tones create a meditative space, suitable for a quiet experience with low arousal; thick coating, dripping, scratching or collage produce strong textures, emphasizing the materiality of the material itself, increasing visual weight and making the mood more tense. Using non-traditional materials (such as sand, wood chips, metal, etc.) creates tactile associations that can heighten or weaken emotions.

- Negative space and texture contrast: In "Blue Sky, Mountain Aspens, and the Roaring Hondo", John Marin juxtaposes smooth colors with rough strokes of dry brushing, using dissonant colors such as mustard yellow against blue-green, indigo against tan, etc. to create friction, and leaving the uncolored paper as negative space. This contrast intensifies the energy of the water flow and the musical push-pull rhythm [^18]. As you look at the work, notice whether there are contrasts between smooth and rough, rich colors and white space, and how they build rhythm and emotional tension.

- Space and shape relationship: Shape distinguishes between geometric and organic. Geometric shapes (square, circle, triangle, etc.) convey rationality and order [^9], while organic shapes are irregular and more natural and uncertain [^9]. Symmetric and repetitive structures create a sense of security, while asymmetric or random arrangements create tension and vitality. The relationship between positive and negative space determines the mood of oppression or openness: different proportions of the subject and blank space will lead to different feelings for the viewer [^9]. Space can be flattened to emphasize surface rhythm, or depth and perspective can be used to guide the eye deeper and change the emotional rhythm.

- Personal interpretation of symbols and symbols: The Detroit Institute of Arts instructs students to use abstract lines and shapes as personal symbols, such as zigzag lines to represent strength, spirals to symbolize growth, repeating dots to represent family, and gentle waves to symbolize peace [^23]. Abstract art does not limit itself to a single meaning, and viewers can relate the symbols to their own experiences. The Georgia O'Keeffe Museum emphasizes that abstract works are meant to "make you feel a certain emotion" and do not necessarily resemble real objects[^24]. As you look at the work, try to look for repetitions of patterns or symbols and think about what identities, memories or emotions they might represent.

## Confusing style

-Abstract Expressionism: Although both belong to the non-representational category, Abstract Expressionism emphasizes large canvases, improvisational movements and inner emotions; while general abstract art may pay more attention to geometric order and formal relationships[^29].
- Cubism: Cubism is still based on real objects and presents multiple perspectives through decomposition and reconstruction; abstract art may be completely divorced from objects and emphasize pure form.

## Output suggestions

To help the model determine emotion, please follow the following structure when answering: 1. List visual evidence: describe what characteristics were observed in terms of color, line, shape/space, composition/materials, and how these characteristics affected your feelings (valence vs. arousal). 2. Reading path: Describe how your eyes move in the picture, whether this movement is slow and coherent or jumpy and intense, and how it affects the emotional rhythm. 3. Comprehensive judgment: Based on the above evidence, give the dominant emotion, valence and arousal of the emotion, and point out the main evidence. The sample output sentence pattern is as follows:

```text
The primary emotion this painting is most likely to evoke is _____. In terms of valence /
arousal, it tends toward _____, with _____ arousal. The basis is not a concrete object, but
the combined effect of ____, ____ and ____ in the image, which creates a _____ viewing
experience. Among these, ____ mainly affects valence, ____ mainly raises / lowers arousal,
and ____ strengthens the overall spatial and emotional atmosphere.
```

Emphasize that judgments are based on the viewer's viewing experience rather than the artist's intentions, and point out areas of uncertainty.
