# New York / East Coast Hard-edge-Adjacent Direction

The New York / East Coast hard-edge-adjacent direction is not a fully independent group. It refers to flat color areas, sharp boundaries, impersonal surfaces, and geometric structures shared by artists such as Ellsworth Kelly and Frank Stella. Britannica's Hard-edge Painting entry provides a base reference for flat color, sharp edges, and impersonal surfaces. This direction often overlaps with Minimalism, Color Field Painting, shaped canvas, and post-painterly abstraction, so classification should be cautious.

## Recognition

Look for whether the edge of the canvas, bands, monochrome panels, geometric cuts, or shaped canvas becomes the main subject. Frank Stella's early black paintings use even bands and unpainted canvas lines to create a direct flat visual event; the Saint Louis Art Museum entry for *The Marriage of Reason and Squalor* is a useful reference. Ellsworth Kelly often reduced traditional image space through monochrome shapes, clear edges, and the relation among shapes themselves. Compared with California hard-edge, the East Coast direction may be closer to Minimalist objecthood and the question of what a painting is as a medium.

## Emotion and Valence/Arousal

This direction often feels colder, more direct, and more impersonal. Black-white stripes, repetition, and hard edges can create order, severity, mechanical rhythm, or slight pressure. Bright single colors and large shapes can feel clean, open, even pleasurable. Arousal depends on contrast, stripe density, shape scale, and viewing distance: dense black-white bands create medium-high arousal; a single large color area may lower arousal while maintaining spatial pressure.

## Common Confusion

The main confusion is Minimalism. If the work emphasizes industrial fabrication, repeated units, object presence, and gallery space, it may lean Minimalist. Tate's Minimalism entry helps identify this objecthood and reduced expression. If the work still centers on internal color boundaries, flat composition, and color-shape relations, it can be analyzed as hard-edge-adjacent. Do not classify only because edges are sharp; consider artist context, material, canvas shape, and objecthood.
