# Realism Appreciation Skill

## Description

Realism in nineteenth-century art emphasized ordinary people, contemporary life, labor, social conditions, and unidealized observation. It moved away from heroic myth and polished fantasy toward the visible world: workers, peasants, city streets, interiors, landscapes, and everyday gestures.

Realism is not the same as technical exactness alone. A work can be highly detailed but still romanticized or academic. Realist emotion comes from attention to lived conditions, material surfaces, social position, and the dignity or burden of ordinary life.

For valence and arousal, Realism often produces restrained, grounded emotion. Labor, poverty, fatigue, and social inequality may lower valence. Calm observation, rural dignity, or domestic intimacy may make valence neutral or gently positive. Arousal is usually low to medium unless conflict, crowding, political subject matter, or extreme detail intensifies the scene.

## Method

1. Identify ordinary subject matter: workers, peasants, streets, interiors, tools, fields, factories, markets, or domestic rooms.
2. Ask whether the image idealizes or observes. Realism tends to reduce heroic beautification.
3. Read social context: class, labor, gender, poverty, urbanization, and modern life matter.
4. Read material specificity: clothing, hands, dirt, tools, surfaces, weather, and light can carry emotion.
5. Separate Realism from Naturalism: Naturalism often stresses scientific observation, environment, and determinism.
6. Separate Realism from Academic Art: polished technique alone is not Realism.
