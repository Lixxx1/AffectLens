# Impasto / Materiality Direction

## Definition and Historical Context

The impasto or materiality direction emphasizes pigment, industrial materials, scratches, covering, cracks, and surface weight. It overlaps with Action Painting, but the focus differs: Action Painting asks how action leaves traces, while the materiality direction asks how materials become emotional and physical experience. MoMA's Abstract Expressionism materials emphasize "processes and materials" as an important entry point for understanding the movement. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/abstract-expressionism) Information on Pollock's `Number 1A, 1948` shows that he worked on the floor and used more fluid commercial enamel paint, allowing bodily movement to be captured directly on the canvas. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/78699) Technical research in Nature Heritage Science further confirms that Pollock gradually introduced household enamel paint in this period and expanded the scale of painting through pouring, dripping, and splashing. [Nature Heritage Science](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-016-0105-2)

This direction can also help interpret de Kooning's repeated application, scraping, covering, and reshaping of surfaces. [MoMA Conservation Project](https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2013/07/18/momas-jackson-pollock-conservation-project-number-1a-1948/) Materials are not neutral media; they are visible evidence of conflict, resistance, time, and physical labor.

## Visual Identification

- **Impasto and accumulation**: Raised pigment, thick edges, and local relief increase tactile weight.
- **Scratching and covering**: Scrape marks, erasure, exposed underlayers, and repeated covering show that the image has undergone struggle and correction.
- **Industrial materials**: Enamel, industrial paint, grit, tar, metal, or other nontraditional materials change the image's associations with smell, speed, and weight.
- **Surface contrast**: Smooth flows set against rough accumulation create clashing rhythms.
- **Bodily index**: Handprints, dragging marks, splatter radii, and traces at the canvas edge can all show physical involvement.

## Emotion and Valence / Arousal

Materiality usually produces medium to high arousal. Thick surfaces and rough textures can make an image feel oppressive, struggling, broken, resistant, or primal; flowing enamel and large drips can produce release, speed, and excitement. Valence is often neutral, negative, or mixed, but not fixed. Bright color, open composition, and rhythmic material layers can make the image feel alive; dark color, blocked space, scratching, and heavy buildup can lower valence.

In output, treat material as emotional evidence: "Thick black paint and scratched cracks increase visual weight, creating pressure and struggle; local bright color still gives the image release, so valence is mixed and arousal is high."

## Common Misreadings

- Do not classify every impasto work as Abstract Expressionism. Expressionism, Art Informel, Tachisme, and Neo-Expressionism can also have strong material qualities.
- Photographs may weaken texture information. If only a low-resolution image is available, state uncertainty in the material judgment.
- Smooth color fields do not mean there is no materiality. Staining, absorption, and transparent layers also need to be observed, though their emotional weight differs.
