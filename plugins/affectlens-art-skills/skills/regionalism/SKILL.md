# Regionalism Appreciation Skill

## Description

American Regionalism was prominent in the 1930s and is associated with artists such as Grant Wood, Thomas Hart Benton, and John Steuart Curry. It emphasized rural life, small towns, farms, local labor, American history, and Midwestern or regional identity, often in contrast to European modernism and urban abstraction.

Emotionally, Regionalism can feel familiar, stable, nostalgic, comic, hardworking, patriotic, critical, or uneasy. Valence depends on whether the scene presents community and dignity or exposes hardship, isolation, and social tension. Arousal is usually low to medium, rising with dramatic weather, muscular movement, satire, or crowd scenes.

## Method

1. Look for American rural or small-town settings, farms, churches, barns, fields, roads, domestic spaces, or local rituals.
2. Read figures as social types and individuals: farmers, workers, families, preachers, townspeople, and historical actors.
3. Read stylization: simplified bodies, rhythmic landscapes, clear contours, and mural-like composition.
4. Read mood carefully. Regionalism can be affectionate, satirical, moralizing, or anxious.
5. Distinguish from Social Realism by less emphasis on urban class conflict and more emphasis on local identity and rural narrative.
