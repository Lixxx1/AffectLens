# New York Dada / Readymade

New York Dada is often associated with Marcel Duchamp, Man Ray, Francis Picabia, and others. Its emphasis is less on wartime photomontage and more on the readymade, mechanical humor, wordplay, and cool challenges to art institutions. MoMA's Dada resources can serve as a reference for this New York context. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/interactives/exhibitions/2008/dadaatmoma/) Duchamp selected, named, signed, or recontextualized ordinary manufactured objects so they became questions about what art is. MoMA treats the readymade as a key Dada strategy that challenges beauty, craft, and originality. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/dada/whatisdada)

When identifying this direction, ask whether the work creates conceptual shock through an ordinary object, machine image, photographic experiment, or linguistic naming. A bottle rack, bicycle wheel, urinal, mechanical diagram, or blankly presented object may not create strong visual drama by itself, but once placed into an art context it attacks traditional art value. The Art Story's readymade / found object definition supports this "selection and contextual shift" judgment. [The Art Story](https://www.theartstory.org/definition/readymade-and-found-object/) New York Dada humor is often cool. It does not necessarily have Berlin Dada's fragments and political anger; it works through naming, inversion, signature, repetition, mechanical image, or photographic experiment to produce cognitive displacement.

Emotionally, a readymade may be visually low-arousal but conceptually medium to high in arousal. The object's cool appearance reduces sensory stimulation, while the question "Does this count as art?" raises cognitive activation. Valence is mixed: viewers may find it funny, feel tricked, become angry, feel confused, or feel suddenly liberated. If the object carries bodily, hygienic, sexual, or institutional implications, valence can become sharper; if it depends more on mechanical wordplay, the emotion is often witty and dry.

The main confusions are Conceptual Art and ordinary found-object display. Conceptual Art after the 1960s more systematically makes language, institutions, and ideas the work's core. New York Dada's readymade belongs to an earlier Dada anti-art context and often carries a stronger prank-like, anti-aesthetic, avant-garde provocation. If an ordinary object is merely decorative and lacks naming, contextual shift, or institutional challenge, do not classify it as Dada. Ask how the object is selected, named, displayed, and how it breaks art expectations.
