# Zurich Dada

Zurich Dada usually begins with Cabaret Voltaire in 1916 and is the earliest, most performance-driven branch of Dada. Tate and Britannica's Dada overviews both support connecting Zurich Dada with World War I, anti-rationality, and anti-art context. [Tate](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/art-terms/d/dada) [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/art/Dada) It formed in neutral Switzerland during World War I. Participants included Hugo Ball, Emmy Hennings, Tristan Tzara, Jean Arp, Sophie Taeuber-Arp, Marcel Janco, and others. Its core is not one unified visual style, but the use of sound poetry, masks, dance, recitation, absurd costumes, improvisation, and irrational language against the collapse of so-called rational civilization.

When identifying Zurich Dada, evidence often comes from performance records, posters, poetry layouts, abstract collage, woodcuts, masks, and stage photographs. Sound poetry is especially important: words may be broken into meaningless syllables so language no longer communicates clear information but becomes rhythm, noise, bodily action, and absurd performance. MoMA's "What is Dada?" supports treating nonsense, chance, anti-art, and performative provocation as Dada evidence. [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/dada/whatisdada)

If a work contains exaggerated costume, geometric masks, broken text, handwritten slogans, improvisational stage feeling, and antiwar context, Zurich Dada is plausible. It is not necessarily as directly political as later Berlin Dada, but its absurdity itself rejects war and bourgeois rationality.

Emotionally, Zurich Dada moves between comedy, confusion, liberation, provocation, and unease. Nonsense language and improvisational performance raise arousal because viewers cannot decode them through ordinary logic. Bright costume and comic movement can briefly push valence positive, but antiwar context and broken linguistic order make valence complex. If the image resembles festival, stage, or game, the emotion may be excitement and release; if the performance appears strange, mechanical, shrill, or masked, it may produce discomfort.

Do not treat Zurich Dada as ordinary avant-garde theater or abstract performance. Its evidence lies in the combination of anti-rationality, anti-art, antiwar context, and linguistic destruction. If the performance aims to build dream imagery, it may be closer to Surrealism. If it is only formal abstract dance without anti-art attitude, do not classify it as Dada too quickly.
